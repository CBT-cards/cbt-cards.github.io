Practice cards & metaphors

Three compact reflection tools built around a mechanism, a small observable action, and a metaphor that helps the idea stick.

Each pair below contains a practice card and a metaphor. The card gives you something concrete to notice or try. The metaphor is only a memory aid; it is not evidence that the idea is correct for every situation.

Prediction → observation

Test Your Prediction

Use it when: an anxious prediction feels like a conclusion even though the outcome has not happened yet.

  1. Write the prediction in one specific sentence.
  2. Rate how likely you think it is from 0 to 100.
  3. Choose one small, safe way to test or observe the prediction.
  4. Decide in advance what you will actually observe instead of guessing what another person thinks.
  5. Afterward, write what happened and rate the prediction again.
  6. Keep the result even if it does not reassure you. The aim is better evidence, not forced optimism.

Small version: one prediction, one observable test, one result.

Reflection: What did the result change, if anything, about how certain you are?

Metaphor: The Weather Forecast

A forecast can be useful without being a fact about what the sky will do. You can prepare for rain and still look outside. An anxious prediction works similarly: notice the forecast, then update it with what actually happens.

The metaphor separates prediction from observation. It does not prove that a feared outcome is safe or impossible.

Boundary

Keep experiments small and ordinary-risk. Do not use this exercise for genuine danger, irreversible or unusually high-stakes actions, or decisions that need medical, legal, financial, safeguarding, or other qualified guidance. Never break a safety rule, law, policy, or professional instruction to “test” a thought.

Sources

The NICE social-anxiety guideline includes behavioral experiments that test negative beliefs within a structured CBT treatment, while the NHS thought-record exercise compares evidence for and against a thought before considering a more realistic or neutral alternative.

  • NICE — Social anxiety disorder: recommendations
  • NHS — Thought record
Action → uncertainty

Problem or Worry?

Use it when: you are thinking about the same concern repeatedly and need to know whether more thinking should produce an action.

  1. Write the worry in one sentence.
  2. Ask: Is there something practical I can do about this now, or schedule to do later?
  3. If yes, name the smallest useful next action and when you will do it.
  4. If no, label it as a hypothetical or currently unsolvable worry instead of continuing to search for a perfect answer.
  5. If useful, capture it for a planned review or worry period, then return attention to the task or moment in front of you.

Small version: classify one worry, then either schedule one action or park it for review.

Reflection: Did more thinking create a usable action, or only another version of the same what-if?

Metaphor: The Toolbox and the Weather

A toolbox is useful for a loose hinge. It is not useful for tomorrow’s weather. Some concerns have handles you can grab and work on; others are uncertainty you can notice without pretending there is a tool that will remove it.

The metaphor helps choose between practical problem solving and tolerating uncertainty. It is not a rule for dismissing real problems.

Boundary

Do not label a concrete problem “hypothetical” just to avoid dealing with it. Urgent safety issues and concerns that need medical, legal, financial, safeguarding, or other professional input still need appropriate action.

Sources

NHS self-help CBT resources explicitly distinguish solvable or practical problems from hypothetical worries and suggest different responses for each.

  • NHS — Problem solving
  • NHS — Tackling your worries
Protection → learning

Spot the Safety Behavior

Use it when: a situation is objectively low risk, but anxiety leads you to rely on an extra routine mainly to prevent embarrassment, uncertainty, or a feared social outcome.

  1. Name the low-risk situation and the feared outcome.
  2. Name the extra behavior you use to prevent, hide, or control that outcome.
  3. Ask whether the behavior protects against real danger or mainly protects against anxiety, embarrassment, or uncertainty.
  4. If it is genuinely protective, keep it.
  5. If it is non-essential and the situation is objectively safe, consider one small reduction rather than removing every coping strategy at once.
  6. Observe what happens without mind reading or grading yourself on how calm you felt.

Small version: identify one non-essential safety-seeking behavior; change nothing until you have checked that it is not a real safety measure.

Reflection: What did the behavior help you avoid learning about the situation?

Metaphor: The Training Wheels

Training wheels can help at first, but if they always touch the ground it is hard to learn what your own balance can do. The useful comparison is not “remove every protection”; it is “on a safe, flat path, is this extra support still teaching you something or preventing you from finding out?”

Genuine safety equipment, protective boundaries, accessibility aids, medication instructions, and professional recommendations are not “training wheels.”

Boundary

This card is deliberately narrow. Do not reduce genuine safety measures or protective boundaries. Do not use it around driving, machinery, hazardous work, medical risk, abuse, intoxication, self-harm risk, or any situation where real-world safety is uncertain. If you are unsure whether a behavior is protective, keep it.

Source

The NICE social-anxiety guideline specifically includes attention to safety-seeking behaviors and behavioral experiments within structured individual CBT. This card borrows that mechanism only for carefully scoped, low-risk reflection; it is not a substitute for treatment.

  • NICE — Social anxiety disorder: recommendations

Clinical boundary

CBT Cards is for general wellness and self-reflection. These prompts are not diagnosis, treatment, emergency support, or individualized medical advice. If a situation involves real danger, severe distress, or a decision that needs professional expertise, the reflection card is not the priority.
